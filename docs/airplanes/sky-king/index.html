How it flies

Paper clips on the nose
Back edges

Snub Nose has a short, squashed nose and two little fins at the back. Throw it up high and it turns into a slow, floating glider.

The tip of the nose is folded back inside and squashed flat. That packs layers of paper at the front, where the weight is needed, and gives a blunt nose that survives bumps. Folded on A4, it should balance about 60 mm behind the nose.

Behind the nose the wing spreads out in a wide triangle. The two fins at the back of the wing tips are its tail: they keep the nose pointing into the air.

Curl the back edges up a little and the nose stays up, so it glides instead of diving. Too much and it climbs, stalls and drops. Our version is untested. Tell us how long it stayed up.

Make it

What you need One rectangular sheet and a ruler. You can start with plain paper.

  1. Step 1 / 13

    Find the centre

    Cut out the sheet along the thick border. Print side down, short edge at the top. Fold in half lengthwise, press the middle crease and open the sheet again.

  2. Step 2 / 13

    Bring the corners to the centre

    Fold both top corners in so their edges meet on the centre crease.

  3. Step 3 / 13

    Point down

    Fold the point down so its tip stops 23.8 mm above the bottom edge.

  4. Step 4 / 13

    Corners in, then open

    Fold the new top corners in to the centre through all layers, crease well and open them again.

  5. Step 5 / 13

    Sides to the creases

    Fold each side edge in so it lies along the crease you just made.

  6. Step 6 / 13

    Corners in again

    Refold the corners on the creases from step 4, carrying the side flaps inside. The two flap edges meet in a slit along the centre.

  7. Step 7 / 13

    Mark the nose

    Fold the nose tip down to the end of the slit, crease and open again.

  8. Step 8 / 13

    Turn over

    Turn the model over, left to right, so the print faces up. The nose still points away.

  9. Step 9 / 13

    Fold in half

    Fold the right half behind the left along the centre. The flaps end up outside.

  10. Step 10 / 13

    Blunt the nose

    Fold the tip back 14.4 mm, crease, open, and push it inside between the layers (an inside reverse fold). The front is now blunt.

  11. Step 11 / 13

    Squash the nose

    Open the reversed tip into a pocket and squash it flat into a small square on the front. Pull the square’s tip down halfway and flatten it again. Its top edge marks where the wings fold.

  12. Step 12 / 13

    Fold one wing, then match its twin

    Fold the upper wing down along the top of the square, parallel to the keel and 15 mm from it. Turn over and fold the other wing to match.

  13. Step 13 / 13

    Fins and elevator

    Fold the outer 13 mm of each wing up into a fin, parallel to the centre line. From behind: a shallow V, fins upright. Curl the back edges up slightly.

Launch it

Practise with a gentle, level push. For time, throw hard nearly straight up.

Trim it

If it…Try this
DivesCurl both back edges up a little.
Climbs, stalls and dropsFlatten the back edges a little.
Turns leftLook from behind and make both wings, both tips and the V match. Then bend the back edge of the right wing up 1 mm.
Turns rightMake both sides match. Then bend the back edge of the left wing up 1 mm.

What to try. These fixes follow how paper gliders behave. They have not yet been tested on this design.

Why it exists

Where it comes from

Takuo Toda of the Japan Origami Airplane Association designs planes folded from one uncut sheet. In 2009 a modified Sky King stayed up for 27.9 s, a Guinness record; his 2010 record of 29.2 s used a newer design, the Sky King Zero Fighter, folded from A5. The current record, 31.2 s from 2026, belongs to another team. Most airtime finalists at Red Bull Paper Wings 2022 flew the Sky King.

Snub Nose is Open Flight’s version of the A4 Sky King. The fold sequence is derived from Toda’s design; the diagrams, text and art are our own, drawn from the fold geometry. Nothing is copied from the videos or books. Guinness and Red Bull appear only as facts; there is no endorsement.

It borrows the squashed nose, the swept wing and the tail fins of the Sky King.

Try this

How much curl does the back edge need?

Predict first. Predict first: flat, slightly curled or strongly curled — which stays up longest?

  1. Fold one Snub Nose with flat back edges.
  2. Launch it five times, gently and level, from the same height. Count the seconds to landing.
  3. Curl both back edges up slightly and launch five more times.
  4. Curl them more and launch five more times.
  5. Compare the middle time of each set of five.

What to look for. Look for dives with flat edges, a long glide with a slight curl, and climbs and stalls with too much.

Go deeper

Snub Nose is 139 mm long and 140 mm across, an aspect ratio of 1.35, with 145 cm² of wing for 4.4 g of paper: about 310 g per square metre, which predicts a glide of 3–4 m/s. The squashed nose stacks eight layers in the first 14 mm. Its leading edges are swept back 45°. The balance point (60 mm) sits just behind the flat-wing neutral point (54 mm) and well ahead of the wing-area centroid (85 mm), so it may be only just stable: one clip moves it to about 55 mm. These figures are estimates from the fold geometry.

For time aloft, Takuo Toda throws nearly straight up and lets the plane level off at the top. Time aloft is roughly the height reached divided by the sink rate, so a plane that climbs straight and then sinks slowly does best.
