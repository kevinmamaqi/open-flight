How it flies

Paper clips on the nose
Back edges

Prairie Skipper has the biggest wing in the fleet for the same sheet of paper. A big, light wing can fly slowly, so it can stay up a long time.

Your throw starts it. Then it glides downhill through the air while weight pulls and drag slows it.

The rolled front edge does two jobs. It makes the front of the wing stiff, and it puts three layers of paper across the front 12 mm.

The thing that matters most is balance. Folded on A4, it should balance about 121 mm behind the nose. Our estimate says that may be too far back for such a broad wing, so keep a paper clip ready. If it climbs and stalls, slide the clip onto the nose before you bend the back edges. This design is untested. Tell us how it flew.

Make it

What you need One rectangular sheet and a ruler. You can start with plain paper.

  1. Step 1 / 7

    Find the centre

    Cut out the sheet along the thick border. Print side up. Put the short edge at the top. Bring the two long edges together, press the middle crease, then open the sheet again.

  2. Step 2 / 7

    Fold a narrow front cuff

    Bring the short top edge down 24 mm to make a 12 mm cuff. Press the whole width.

  3. Step 3 / 7

    Roll the cuff once more

    Roll the same strip towards you over its lower edge. The new front sits 24 mm from the original top. Both folds should be parallel.

  4. Step 4 / 7

    Fold in the shoulders

    On each side fold the cuff corner diagonally inwards on line 4. The marked diagonal joins two points 56 mm from that corner. A straight strip stays across the middle of the nose.

  5. Step 5 / 7

    Close the body

    Keep the folded details face up. Fold the right half away from you, behind the left, along the centre line. The folded details will be on the outside.

  6. Step 6 / 7

    Fold one wing, then match its twin

    Hold the closed body with the centre edge on your right. Fold only the upper wing outward on the line 16 mm from that edge. Turn the model over and fold the other wing to match. From behind, the wings make a T with the body hanging below.

  7. Step 7 / 7

    Raise the wing tips

    Lift each wingtip about 10 mm above the body fold. Turn up the outer 8 mm of each long side until both tips stand upright. Sight from the tail and match the heights.

Launch it

Pinch the body under the wings. Hold it at shoulder height with the nose level or a little down. Push it forward gently, at walking speed, and let it find its own speed.

Trim it

If it…Try this
Swoops and stallsSlide one paper clip onto the nose. It should then balance about 110 mm behind the nose (estimate).
Turns or drops a wing right after launchSight along the front edge. A twisted cuff steers the plane, so flatten it before you bend anything else.
Dives into the floorBend up the back 15 mm of each wing, next to the body, until the back corner sits 1 mm above the wing. Still diving: bend 1 mm more.
Rolls over and spirals downLift each tip to about 10 mm above the body fold, both at the same height.
Rocks from side to sideFlatten the V a little and check that the tips stand upright.

What to try. These fixes follow how paper gliders behave. They have not yet been tested on this design.

Why it exists

Why it exists

Rolling the front edge over itself is a common trick in paper gliders. It makes a stiff, heavy front edge. Prairie Skipper also folds in its shoulders, which leaves a broad, almost square wing.

Its name points to the Douglas DC-3, a 1930s airliner that helped airlines earn money by carrying passengers. The first version flew on 17 December 1935, with beds for overnight passengers. The likeness is in spirit only. The DC-3’s wing is long and slender, about nine times as long as it is wide. The paper wing is shorter than the plane is long.

It borrows the DC-3’s gentle, patient flying, not its long, slender wing.

Try this

Which one sinks more slowly?

Predict first. Predict first: after a gentle launch, which stays up longer, Prairie Skipper or Delta Comet? Our prediction is Prairie Skipper, if it is well balanced. Its wing is 60 % bigger for the same sheet, so it can fly more slowly.

  1. Fold a Prairie Skipper and a Delta Comet from the same paper.
  2. Balance each on a pencil. Add a clip to the Skipper if it balances behind 121 mm.
  3. Launch each five times from the same height, with the same gentle push.
  4. Time each flight with a stopwatch, from release to the first touch.
  5. Write down every stall.

What to look for. Look for a slow, flat glide. If it swoops and stalls, add a clip and try again.

Go deeper

Prairie Skipper’s aspect ratio, a measure of how long and slim a wing is, is 0.61, about a fifteenth of the real DC-3’s 9 (29 m span, 92 m² of wing). Each square metre of wing carries about 123 g (1.2 N/m²), the least of the four, so the model predicts a glide of roughly 1.8–2.6 m/s. The balance point sits 44 % of the way along the wing’s average depth. A simple flat-wing model puts the neutral point, the spot where extra lift acts when the nose tips up, at 46 mm, and the middle of the wing area at 137 mm. So the plane can only be steady if swirls of air along its side edges add lift further back when it flies nose-high. Expect it to fly slowly and nose-high, and to want weight on the nose: one clip should bring the balance to about 110 mm. The 8 mm tips stop air spilling round the wing ends and work as small fins behind the balance point. All of these figures are estimates from the fold geometry.

Contest category: airtime. Contests ask for an uncut sheet, so practise with the whole-sheet build, not the cut border. At home, time gentle launches; a broad, soft wing like this one may not survive a hard throw.
