How it flies

Paper clips on the nose
Back edges

Throw Homecoming on its side, wings standing up, and it flies round in a circle and comes back towards you.

Wings push the plane the way they face. Flat wings push it up. Turn the wings on their side and the push points sideways, into the middle of a circle.

Bending the back edges up makes the wings work harder, and the circle gets smaller. That is how you tune it: with the back edges, not with a harder throw.

The winglets at the back keep the nose pointing along the circle. Folded on A4, it should balance about 74 mm behind the nose. Our version is untested. Tell us how close it came back.

Make it

What you need One rectangular sheet and a ruler. You can start with plain paper.

  1. Step 1 / 10

    Find the centre

    Cut out the sheet along the thick border. Print side up, short edge at the top. Fold the sheet in half lengthwise, behind, and open it again.

  2. Step 2 / 10

    Corners in, 1 mm short

    Fold each top corner down so its top edge stops 1 mm short of the centre crease. A 2 mm flat stays at the top.

  3. Step 3 / 10

    Tip down to the mark

    Mark the centre crease 50.5 mm from the bottom edge. Fold the whole top down so the tip lands on the mark.

  4. Step 4 / 10

    New corners in, then open

    Fold the two new top corners in to 1 mm from the centre, through all layers. Crease firmly and open them again.

  5. Step 5 / 10

    Outer edges to the creases

    Fold each long outer edge in to the crease you just made, stopping 1 mm short of it. The lower part of the sheet stays flat.

  6. Step 6 / 10

    Fold the corners in again

    Refold on the creases from step 4, carrying the edge strips inside. The leading edges are now doubled.

  7. Step 7 / 10

    Lock the flaps

    Fold the small tip that shows below the flaps up as far as it goes and tuck it under their edges. The flaps no longer spring open.

  8. Step 8 / 10

    Close the body

    Fold the right half behind the left along the centre crease. The flaps stay on the outside.

  9. Step 9 / 10

    Fold one wing, then match its twin

    Mark 9.3 mm from the centre fold at the nose and 24 mm at the tail. Fold the upper wing down on that line against a ruler. Turn over and fold the other wing to match.

  10. Step 10 / 10

    Winglets and tail

    Fold the outer 10 mm of each wing back up into a winglet, against a ruler. Bend both back edges up for the elevator: the more bend, the tighter it circles.

Launch it

Hold it with the wings vertical. Turn your body and release it sideways at chest height.

Trim it

If it…Try this
Does not come backBend both back edges up. More bend, smaller circle.
Dives into the floorFlatten the wings, then bend the back edges up a little more.
Rolls over and spirals downLift each tip to about 10 mm above the body fold, both at the same height.

What to try. These fixes follow how paper gliders behave. They have not yet been tested on this design.

Why it exists

Where it comes from

Seunghoon Lee, a science teacher from South Korea, won the aerobatics final of Red Bull Paper Wings on 14 May 2022 with a one-minute routine, and this boomerang came back to his hand. We found no named designer: his channel WePlay published the same fold in 2018, and “Crescent Boomerang” appears to be Foldable Flight’s name for it.

Homecoming is Open Flight’s version of it. The fold sequence is derived from that design; the diagrams, text and art are our own, drawn from the fold geometry. Nothing is copied from the videos. Red Bull appears only as a fact; there is no endorsement.

It borrows the locked, doubled nose, the slanted wing roots and the winglets of the Crescent Boomerang.

Try this

Does the tail set the size of the circle?

Predict first. Predict first: with both back edges bent up, will the circle get bigger or smaller?

  1. Fold one Homecoming and stand at a mark on the floor.
  2. Throw it on its side five times with flat back edges. Mark each landing.
  3. Bend both back edges up along their whole length.
  4. Throw five more times the same way and mark the landings.
  5. Measure how far each mark is from where you stood.

What to look for. Look for landings closer to your mark with the bent tail, and for dives if the wings are not flat.

Go deeper

Turned on its side, all the lift becomes the inward force of a circle: ½ρv²·S·CL = m·v²/r. The speed cancels, so r = 2m / (ρ·S·CL). With 4.4 g of paper and 164 cm² of wing, a lift coefficient of 0.6–1.0 gives a radius of about 0.45–0.75 m. The circle depends on mass, wing area and how hard the wing works, which the bent-up tail sets, not on how hard you throw.

Homecoming is 167 mm long and 143 mm across, an aspect ratio of 1.24. Its balance point (74 mm) lies near the front of the estimated centre-of-lift range (68–104 mm), so its pitch stability is modest; one clip moves the balance to about 68 mm. The bent-up tail does two jobs: it holds the nose up so the plane does not dive, and it sets the size of the circle. These figures are estimates from the fold geometry.
