How it flies

Paper clips on the nose
Back edges

Sky Raft has two jobs: shoot up like a stick when you throw it, then float down like a raft on a slow river.

Almost half of the sheet is rolled into a thick bar along the front edge. That puts the weight at the front, so a hard throw sends it up straight instead of looping.

Behind the bar is a short, wide, nearly square wing with a fin at each tip. Light for its size, it sinks slowly. The fins work like a tail and keep it pointing into the air.

Folded on A4, it should balance about 26 mm behind the front edge. Our estimate puts that close to its limit, so keep a paper clip ready. If it climbs, stalls and drops, clip the middle of the bar. Our version is untested. Tell us how long it stayed up.

Make it

What you need One rectangular sheet and a ruler. You can start with plain paper.

  1. Step 1 / 13

    Find the centre

    Cut out the sheet along the thick border. Print side up, short edge at the top. Fold in half lengthwise, press the middle crease and open the sheet again.

  2. Step 2 / 13

    Corners in, 1 mm short

    Fold each top corner down so its edge stops 1 mm short of the centre crease. A 2 mm gap stays between them.

  3. Step 3 / 13

    Point down

    Fold the top point down to where the two corner tips meet. The fold runs 48.5 mm below the old top edge.

  4. Step 4 / 13

    Fold the edge down twice

    Divide the distance from the top edge to the point into thirds. Fold the top edge down on the first third, then fold the band over once more along its lower edge.

  5. Step 5 / 13

    Open everything

    Unfold the sheet completely. The band has left short creases across the corner flaps; the innermost pair is about 18 mm either side of the centre.

  6. Step 6 / 13

    Pre-crease the wing roots

    Fold a long crease 18 mm either side of the centre, parallel to it, for the full length of the sheet. Fold each one behind, then open.

  7. Step 7 / 13

    Refold the nose

    Repeat the corners, the point and the two band folds on the creases you already have.

  8. Step 8 / 13

    Start rolling

    Mark 104.8 mm up from the bottom edge on both sides. Roll the whole nose down tightly, keeping the root creases of every layer lined up.

  9. Step 9 / 13

    Roll to the mark

    Keep rolling. The last roll must land exactly on the marks: five rolls in all. A 104.8 mm deep wing is left, with a thick bar along its front.

  10. Step 10 / 13

    Chamfer the tips

    Mark 25.2 mm in from each front corner along the bar and 14.9 mm down the side. Fold each corner in on the line between the marks.

  11. Step 11 / 13

    Fold in half

    Fold the right half over onto the left along the centre crease, the bar inside.

  12. Step 12 / 13

    Wings out

    Fold the upper wing back out on its root crease, 18 mm from the centre fold, through the nose layers. Turn over and fold the other wing to match.

  13. Step 13 / 13

    Fold the fins

    Fold the outer 15 mm of each wing up into a fin, over the folded corner, against a ruler so it is straight. Give the wings a slight V and curl the back edges up a little.

Launch it

Start with gentle throws at about 45°. Once it glides well, throw harder and steeper.

Trim it

If it…Try this
Dives after the climbCurl the back edge up 1 mm, then 2 mm. Stand the fins straight.
Turns leftLook from behind and make both wings, both tips and the V match. Then bend the back edge of the right wing up 1 mm.
Turns rightMake both sides match. Then bend the back edge of the left wing up 1 mm.

What to try. These fixes follow how paper gliders behave. They have not yet been tested on this design.

Why it exists

Where it comes from

Rana Muhammad Usman Saeed from Pakistan won the airtime final of Red Bull Paper Wings in May 2022 with a square glider: 14.86 s. His 16.39 s throw the day before is the championship’s Guinness record, not the overall time-aloft record. Foldable Flight credits the design to Shin Moo Joon, inspired by Ken Blackburn’s glider, and calls it the “Shaheen Shin”; we found no other source for the name.

Sky Raft is Open Flight’s version of it. The fold sequence is derived from that design; the diagrams, text and art are our own, drawn from the fold geometry. Nothing is copied from the videos. Red Bull and Guinness appear only as facts; there is no endorsement.

It borrows the rolled front bar, the square wing and the tip fins of the Shaheen Shin.

Try this

Does a curled back edge keep it up longer?

Predict first. Predict first: will a 2 mm curl at the back edge make it glide longer, or make it stall?

  1. Fold one Sky Raft.
  2. Launch it five times, gently and level, from the same height. Count the seconds to landing.
  3. Curl the back edge up by 2 mm along its whole width.
  4. Launch it five more times the same way and count again.
  5. Compare the middle time of each set of five.

What to look for. Look for a flatter, longer glide with the curl, and for bobbing or stalls if the curl is too big.

Go deeper

Sky Raft is 130 mm across and 105 mm deep on A4, an aspect ratio of 1.25, with 136 cm² of wing for 4.4 g of paper: about 330 g per square metre, which predicts a glide of 3–4 m/s. The rolled bar stacks up to 11 layers near the centre. A flat-wing model puts the neutral point 20 mm behind the front edge; the side-edge vortices of a short wing move the real centre of lift back towards the area centroid at 53 mm (Polhamus; Torres and Mueller). The balance at 26 mm lies inside that range, near its front end, so the plane may be barely stable: a clip moves it to about 25 mm, two clips to 23 mm. These figures are estimates from the fold geometry.

Time aloft is roughly launch height divided by sink rate. An airtime plane therefore needs two opposite things: compact and nose-heavy for the climb, light and broad for the descent. A slight V from behind and a small upward curl at the back edge help it settle into a wide, gentle circle.
