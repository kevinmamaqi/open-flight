Chapter 9

Contests and records

How people measure a paper flight, and how to hold a contest at home.

People have thrown paper planes for distance and for time for decades, and the records keep growing. This chapter shows how a record is measured, who holds the records today and how to run a fair contest of your own.

9.1 What counts as a record

A record is not just a long throw. It is a long throw that was measured under rules and checked by someone else.

Records need categories. The two best known are distance, how far a plane flies from the throwing line to the spot where it first touches the ground, and time aloft, how long it stays in the air after it leaves the hand. There are others. Guinness World Records also lists the fastest time to fold and throw a paper plane, and the largest paper plane ever flown. The current one flew in Bologna, Italy, on 25 June 2026, with wings 20.04 m from tip to tip. It beat an 18.21 m plane built by students in Braunschweig, Germany1,2.

Records need rules. Is the paper A4, or a bigger sheet? May you cut it or add tape? Is the throw made indoors, where no wind can help? Change any rule and you have a different record, so record books state the rules alongside the number.

Records need measurement. Distance is measured along the ground, with a long tape or a laser. Time is measured with a stopwatch or a video, from the release to the first touch.

Records need evidence. In February 2012 a plane designed by John Collins and thrown by Joe Ayoob flew 69.14 m in a hangar in California. Guinness officials were not there, and the first Guinness report said the evidence was still to be reviewed3. Guinness now lists that flight in its record history4. A witness, a video and a careful log turn a great throw into a record.

The same habits make a good experiment. Chapter 10 uses them at home.

Try it

  1. Choose one category: distance or time.
  2. Write down three rules before anyone throws.
  3. Decide who measures, and how.
  4. Keep every throw on the list, even the bad ones.

Go deeper

Guinness’s public record pages give the result, the people, the place and the date, but not the full rules5,6. Guinness’s own history of the distance record gives its main rules: one sheet of A4 lighter than 100 g/m², flown indoors in still air, launched from a standing start behind a line, with a walk or run-up of up to 3.04 m4.

Contests set their own rules, which differ again: see Red Bull Paper Wings on the next spreads7.

9.2 Records over time

Here are the longest paper-plane flights, drawn to scale. In the book, one millimetre on the page is one metre on the floor.

In 1985 the longest recorded paper-plane flight was 58.82 m. Forty years later it is 98.43 m, set in Shanghai on 28 December 2025 by a team of six. That is almost the length of a football pitch4,5.

The record grew in steps. John Collins describes one of them: earlier record planes were darts thrown hard and high, while his 2012 plane was thrown level and flew as a glider8. In 2022 the Daegu team split the work, with one person designing, one folding and one throwing9. Every record since has been set by a team.

Time aloft has grown more slowly: from 27.6 s in 1998 to 31.2 s in 2026, less than four seconds in almost thirty years20,6. Every extra second is hard to win, because a paper plane can stay up only as long as its height lasts. Count slowly to thirty-one: that is how long the record plane stayed in the air.

9.3 Red Bull Paper Wings

The largest paper-plane contest so far has three events: distance, airtime and aerobatics.

Red Bull Paper Wings is a paper-plane championship run by the drinks company Red Bull. Guinness World Records says it was first held in 2006 and returns every three years11. The world finals have taken place in Hangar-7 in Salzburg, Austria, an indoor hall with no wind12.

In 2022 more than 61,000 people in over 60 countries entered more than 500 local rounds, and the best of them flew in the sixth world final at Hangar-712. Guinness counted 61,210 entrants, the largest paper aircraft competition it has recorded11.

Each event had its own rules. For distance and airtime, every pilot folded one A4 sheet supplied on site, with no cutting, tearing, glue, staples or extra weight. Distance throws were made from behind a line, and the better of two throws counted. Airtime throws were made standing still with both feet on the ground, and the clock ran from the release to the first touch. In aerobatics a jury scored each display for how engaging, how well flown and how creative it was7.

Watch the distance grow. Jovica Kozlica of Croatia won the distance title in 2006 with 39.43 m, and won it again in 2009 with 54.43 m24. In 2022 Lazar Krstić of Serbia won with 61.11 m, about five buses parked nose to tail estimate25.

In the 2022 airtime event, Rana Muhammad Usman Saeed of Pakistan kept a plane up for 16.39 s in an early round, a championship record, and won the final with 14.86 s26. Seunghoon Lee of South Korea won aerobatics12. In 2019 Kateryna Ahafonova of Ukraine became the first woman to win a title, in aerobatics13.

We found no world final after 2022, as of 25 November 2026.

Try it

  1. Fold your best plane from one plain A4 sheet. No cutting and no tape.
  2. Make two airtime throws, standing still with both feet down.
  3. A helper times each one from release to first touch.

Look for: Keep the better time.

Go deeper

Guinness keeps three records of the championship itself: the largest competition, the farthest throw and the longest time aloft11,25,26. Five buses assumes a 12 m city bus estimate.

Contest results sit well below the records: 61.11 m in the 2022 final, against the 77.134 m record set that April. A contest gives every pilot the same paper and two throws on the day, while a record team can prepare for months and choose its paper.

Open Flight is not connected with Red Bull, and this spread describes the contest without endorsing it. Check the current rules before entering any contest.

9.4 John M. Collins

Paper airplane designer · distance record, 2012

He brought origami technique to paper planes, and showed that a plane thrown level and flown as a glider could beat the hard-thrown dart.

Guinness World Records described John Collins in 2012 as a television producer and origami enthusiast3. In a WIRED video he says he spent about ten years learning origami before he took its techniques back to paper airplanes8.

On 26 February 2012, in a hangar at McClellan in Sacramento, California, a plane he designed was thrown 69.14 m by Joe Ayoob3. It beat the 63.19 m that Stephen Krieger, then 15, threw in a hangar in Washington State in September 20034,18. Collins named the plane Suzanne, after his wife8.

In the same video he explains what changed. Earlier record planes were darts, thrown hard at about 45 degrees to fly a high arc. His plane was thrown level and flew as a glider. He designs for ordinary US Letter paper and stresses accurate folds, symmetry and test flights8.

In 2020 he used five of his designs in another WIRED video to explain aerodynamics, from dihedral and up-elevator to wing loading and glide ratio14. His account of lift there is one simplified view; Chapter 2 sets out how scientists describe it.

His book, The New World Champion Paper Airplane Book, was published by Ten Speed Press in 2013 and includes his instructions for Suzanne15.

WIRED also filmed him folding his planes step by step, among them the Phoenix and the Tube28,29,30.

We do not reproduce his designs, his diagrams or their names. To fold them, use his book, his videos and his website16.

9.5 The Daegu team, 2022

Distance record holders, April to December 2022

Three people, three jobs: one designed the plane, one folded it and one threw it.

On 16 April 2022, in Daegu, South Korea, a paper plane flew 77.134 m and broke a record that had stood for ten years. Guinness World Records credits three people: Kim Kyu Tae of South Korea threw it, Shin Moo Joon folded it, and Chee Yie Jian of Malaysia, known as Julian, designed it9.

Eight of their throws were measured. Even the shortest, 71.813 m, was longer than the old record9.

The paper was Conqueror CX22 at 100 g/m², the same stock John Collins had used9. That detail matters: when the rules allow it, choosing the paper is part of the design.

Their record lasted less than eight months. On 2 December 2022 Dillon Ruble, with Nathaniel Erickson and Garrett Jensen, reached 88.31 m in Crown Point, Indiana, USA, after four months of preparation, with a design they said was inspired by hypersonic vehicles10. All three had studied aerospace or mechanical engineering, and news reports at the time called them Boeing engineers23.

On 28 December 2025 Liu Liwen and five teammates reached 98.43 m in Shanghai5. Guinness notes that record teams usually keep their folds secret, and that design has not been published4.

Several members of that team also appear in the time-aloft record, 31.2 s, set in Kunshan on 11 February 20266.

9.6 Ken Blackburn and Takuo Toda

Time-aloft records · 1998 to 2026

Distance needs a hard, flat throw. Time needs height and the slowest possible fall. For almost thirty years the time record has moved in tenths of a second.

A time plane is thrown upwards, to turn the speed of the arm into height. At the top it must level out and sink as slowly as it can. Broad wings and careful trim matter more than raw speed.

Ken Blackburn of St. Louis, in the United States, kept a paper plane up indoors for 27.6 seconds20. His mark, set in 1998, lasted eleven years21.

On 11 April 2009, at a contest in Fukuyama, Japan, Takuo Toda beat it by three tenths of a second, with 27.9 s21. In December 2010 he reached 29.2 s2. That record stood for more than fifteen years.

Toda leads Japan’s Origami Airplane Association. At the national contest it ran with Japan Airlines in March 2018, every pilot folded one sheet of the association’s contest paper, using folds only, and the clock ran from release to landing22. A 15-year-old won the open class with 17.53 s. A 12-year-old won the primary-school class with 14.75 s, just a tenth of a second short of the time that won the 2022 Red Bull world final.

On 11 February 2026, in Kunshan, China, a team of seven reached 31.2 s6. Some of them had also thrown the 98.43 m distance record.

9.7 Contests around the world

Paper-plane contests have run for more than half a century, from a magazine challenge in New York to a race against the clock in Japan.

In 1967 the magazine Scientific American ran a paper-plane contest. The final fly-offs were held in the Great Hall of the New York Hall of Science, a building first made to show rockets at the 1964 World’s Fair. In 2008 a new contest came back to the same hall27.

In Japan, the Origami Airplane Association and Japan Airlines have run contests for time aloft, with classes split by age and one sheet of contest paper, folded only17,22.

The biggest so far is Red Bull Paper Wings, earlier in this chapter. In 2022 it drew 61,210 entrants, the largest paper-plane competition Guinness has recorded11.

The quickest contest of all is a Guinness record. Start with a flat A4 sheet, fold it to a set diagram and make it fly at least 2 m. In 2017 the record was 35.4 seconds. In April 2026 Ikai Tomoki of Japan did it in 4.54 seconds4. Count to five, slowly: his plane was folded and flying before you finish.

Schools, clubs and museums run contests too, each with its own rules. Before you enter one, read them: paper, cuts, tape and the launch all differ.

Try it

  1. A helper holds a stopwatch and says go.
  2. Fold a simple dart from a flat A4 sheet, as fast as you can.
  3. Throw it past a line 2 m away. The helper stops the clock as it crosses.
  4. Try three times with the same design.

Look for: Did your fastest fold also fly well, or did speed cost you a good glide?

Go deeper

Guinness’s fold-and-throw rules: a standard A4 sheet, unfolded at the start, folded to a diagram provided, and a flight of at least 2 m. The first record, in May 2017, was 35.435 s by Lu Yu of China4.

TIME reported 10,941 entries from 49 US states and 26 other countries, the same figures as in “A short history of flight”19. Scientific American’s 2008 report mentions the 1967 contest only briefly.

JAL’s first national contest was held on 18 March 2018 in Ōta, Tokyo, the home ward of Haneda Airport22. Open Flight is not connected with any of these contests.

9.8 Throwing for distance, launching for time

A throw for distance and a launch for time are two different skills. Practise both, safely.

For distance, the plane needs speed and a long, shallow path. Hold it under the body, near the balance point. Step forwards and throw firmly in a straight line. Try a level release first, the way glider-style record planes are thrown, then a little higher, and compare.

For time, the plane needs height and a slow sink. Thrown upwards, it climbs for as long as its speed lasts. Then it must level out and glide down slowly. A plane that climbs well but cannot level out will loop or stall, so a good time plane has broad wings, a little up-elevator and a balance point set for a slow glide.

Practise one thing at a time. A small change in your release can matter more than a change to the plane, so keep the throw the same while you trim, and keep the plane the same while you practise the throw.

Safety comes first. Paper planes have pointed noses. Throw only when the lane ahead is clear, never towards anyone’s face, and keep watchers behind the throwing line. Do not climb on furniture to launch higher: a tall helper can hold the plane up high instead. Outdoors, keep away from roads and water.

Try it

  1. Mark a throwing line with tape.
  2. Throw the same plane five times for distance and five times for time.
  3. Write down every result.

Look for: Is the throw that flies farthest also the one that stays up longest?

Go deeper

Forty-five degrees is the best angle for a ball thrown where there is no air. A paper plane makes lift, so its best launch angle depends on the plane: the old record darts were thrown high, while Collins’s record glider was thrown level8.

For time, the throw turns speed into height. A launch at speed v can add at most v²/2g of height, about 5 m for a throw at 10 m/s, and drag takes a share of that estimate.

9.9 Run your own contest

You need paper, masking tape, a tape measure, a stopwatch and a few friends.

This is a family and classroom version of the contests in this chapter. It is not an official Red Bull or Guinness event, and you can change any rule, as long as everyone agrees before the first throw.

Distance

  1. One plain sheet each: A4 or US Letter office paper, 80 g/m². Fold only, with no cutting or tape.
  2. Throw from behind a masking-tape line. A foot over the line means the throw does not count.
  3. Two throws each. The longer one counts.
  4. Measure to the spot where the plane first touches the floor.

Time aloft

  1. The same paper and folding rules.
  2. Stand still with both feet on the floor. No run-up.
  3. Two timers start at the release and stop at the first touch of the floor, a wall or anything else. Use the average of their two times.
  4. Two throws each. The longer one counts.

Aerobatics

  1. Any paper plane you folded yourself.
  2. Each pilot has 60 seconds to show their best flights.
  3. Three judges each give 1 to 10 points for three things: how fun it is to watch, how well it flies and how inventive it is.

In a classroom

  • Run a first round for everyone, then a final for the top three in each event.
  • Give younger and older pupils separate categories.
  • Print one contest sheet, L3, per group, and keep every throw.

The two-throw, best-counts format and the standing airtime launch follow Red Bull’s 2022 rules7. The age split follows the airtime contests that Japan Airlines announced in 2017 with the International Paper Plane Association, as JAL names it17. Two timers average out some of the error in human reaction time.
