Chapter 10

The flight lab

Predict, launch, change one thing, compare.

Scientists ask a question, make a prediction, test it fairly and write down what happens. You can do the same with one sheet of paper, a tape measure and a pencil.

Go deeper

Each experiment changes one thing and keeps the rest the same. We have not yet run them on this book’s designs, so every “What we know so far” says not yet measured. Your log could be the first.

10.1 A fair test

Change one thing. Keep everything else the same. Launch five times.

Suppose you want to know whether a paper clip on the nose makes your plane fly farther. You launch it once without the clip and once with it. The second flight is longer. Did the clip do it?

Maybe. But maybe you threw harder the second time, or a door opened, or the plane landed on a rug. One launch of each cannot tell you. A fair test rules out the other reasons.

First, predict. Say or write what you think will happen, and why. A prediction is not a guess you have to get right. It is how you notice when something surprises you.

Next, keep everything the same except the one thing you are testing: the same plane, the same spot, the same height, the same kind of launch and the same person launching.

Then repeat. Paper planes vary from flight to flight, so launch five times for each setup and compare the middle results, not the single best one.

Last, write everything down, including the strange flights. A flight that hit the lamp is still a result: it tells you about the launch, if not about the clip.

Try it

  1. Choose one question from the next pages.
  2. Write your prediction before the first launch.
  3. Launch five times, change one thing, then launch five more.

Go deeper

This is the control of variables taught in school science: one thing changed on purpose, one result measured, and everything else held constant.

Five launches per setup is a practical minimum for paper planes. With fewer, one lucky throw can decide the answer. The middle value of five cannot be moved by a single odd flight.

10.2 Keeping a flight log

A flight log is a table with one row for each launch. It turns flights into results.

The Print & Play log, sheet L1, has the columns shown here. Before you launch, write the model, the date and your prediction. Then fill in one row for each launch.

Measure the distance to the spot where the plane first touches the floor, not where it slides to. Mark the spot with a small piece of tape, measure it, then pick the tape up.

Write what each flight did in a word or two: glided, dived, stalled, turned left, rolled. These words help you trim later.

Note the conditions too: indoors or outdoors, the paper and any trim you made. If you change something, start a new block of rows.

The log on this page is empty on purpose. We will print a filled-in log only when it comes from a real session, with its date and trial number.

10.3 Where should the clip go?

Does a paper clip on the nose make a plane fly farther?

Why it matters

A clip moves the balance point. Ahead of the neutral point the plane flies steadily; too far ahead it dives. This experiment looks for the best place on one design, and shows the balance rule from Chapter 2 at work.

You need

  • One folded plane, such as plate A1
  • One standard paper clip
  • A tape measure, a pencil and sheet L2

Steps

  1. Predict: will the clip help, hurt or make no difference?
  2. Launch five times with no clip. Record each distance.
  3. Slide the clip onto the nose. Launch five more times the same way.
  4. Move the clip 1 cm further back. Launch five more times.
  5. Find the middle distance of each set of five.
  6. Compare the three middles.

What we know so far: Not yet measured not yet measured. Our model predicts that Upper Deck, plate A1, balances about 104 mm behind the nose with one clip estimate.

Safety: Launch away from people and keep the landing area clear.

10.4 How much up-elevator?

How far should you bend the back edges up?

Why it matters

Bending the back edges up tips the nose up, so the wing meets the air at a bigger angle of attack. A little gives a slower, flatter glide. Too much makes the plane swoop, stall and dive. This test looks for the best bend on one plane.

You need

  • One folded plane, such as plate A1
  • A ruler marked in millimetres
  • A tape measure, a pencil and sheet L2

Steps

  1. Predict which bend will fly farthest.
  2. Flatten both back edges. Launch five times, gently and level, and record each distance.
  3. Bend both back edges up 1 mm. Launch five times.
  4. Repeat at 2 mm and 3 mm, five launches each.
  5. Find the middle distance for each bend.
  6. Tally what each flight did: dive, glide or swoop.

10.5 How big a V?

Does a deeper V make a plane fly straighter?

Why it matters

Dihedral steadies the roll, but too much makes a plane rock. Somewhere between flat and steep is a best angle for each design, and a ruler is enough to find it.

You need

  • One folded plane
  • A ruler
  • Masking tape for a straight line on the floor
  • A tape measure, a pencil and sheet L2

Steps

  1. Predict which V will fly straightest.
  2. Lay a strip of tape straight ahead from your launch spot.
  3. Set the wings flat, with both tips at body height. Launch five times.
  4. Raise both tips 5 mm. Launch five times.
  5. Keep raising the tips in 5 mm steps up to 20 mm, with five launches at each step.
  6. For each launch, measure how far it lands to the left or right of the tape.

Safety: Launch away from people and keep the landing area clear.

10.6 Bigger or smaller wings?

If you trim the wingtips shorter, does the plane fly farther?

Why it matters

A smaller wing has less area to make lift, so the plane must fly faster to hold itself up. Trimming the tips also makes the plane a little lighter and moves its balance point, but the wing loses area faster than the plane loses weight. Faster could mean farther, or a steeper dive. Only a test can tell.

You need

  • One folded plane with broad wings
  • Scissors and a ruler
  • A tape measure, a pencil and sheet L2

Steps

  1. Predict: farther, shorter or the same?
  2. Measure the span from tip to tip. Launch five times and record each distance.
  3. Cut 1 cm off each wingtip, keeping both sides the same. Launch five times.
  4. Cut another 1 cm off each tip. Launch five times.
  5. Find the middle distance for each wing size.
  6. Compare the three middles.

Record your launches on sheet L2

2 cm off each tip

What to look for: Watch the speed and the path, not only the distance. Does the smaller wing fly faster? Does it dive?

What we know so far: Not yet measured not yet measured.

Safety: Cut away from your fingers and your body. Launch away from people.

10.7 Which paper: 80, 120 or 160 g/m²?

Does heavier paper make a better glider?

Why it matters

Heavier paper is stiffer, but it is also heavier. Stiffness keeps the wings in shape; weight makes the plane fly faster. The best paper is a balance between the two, and it may differ between a plane and a butterfly.

You need

  • Three sheets of the same size: 80, 120 and 160 g/m²
  • A printer, or a template to trace
  • A tape measure, a pencil and sheet L2

Steps

  1. Predict which paper will glide farthest.
  2. Fold the same design from each sheet, as alike as you can.
  3. Balance and trim each one until it glides straight.
  4. Launch each five times from the same spot.
  5. Record the distances and find the middle of each set.

Record your launches on sheet L2

80 g/m²

What to look for: Do the light wings bend in flight? Does the heavy plane need a harder throw?

What we know so far: Not yet measured not yet measured. Our model predicts that a paper butterfly’s wingtip bends about 19 mm on 80 g/m² paper and about 8 mm on 120 g/m² estimate.

Safety: Check that your printer takes heavier paper. Most feed 120 g/m² from the normal tray; 160 g/m² may need the manual feed.

10.8 How big a ring?

Can a loop of paper glide farther when you make the ring smaller?

Why it matters

Tucking the ends further in makes the ring smaller and its seam stiffer, and moves more paper to the front. Each change pulls in a different direction, so only a test can tell which wins.

You need

  • Ring glider sheet F1, or one plain sheet
  • A ruler
  • A tape measure, a pencil and sheet L2

Steps

  1. Fold the ring glider, plate F1.
  2. Measure across the opening and write it down.
  3. Launch it five times with a gentle, level toss.
  4. Tuck the ends 1 cm further in. Measure the opening again.
  5. Launch five more times from the same spot.
  6. Compare the middle distances.

Record your launches on sheet L2

Opening12345Middle
First size: ___ mm
Smaller: ___ mm

What to look for: Does the smaller ring fly straighter, farther, or both?

What we know so far: Not yet measured not yet measured. NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory uses a paper ring glider in its classroom projects4.

Safety: Toss gently and away from people.

10.9 Launch height and distance

If you launch from twice as high, does the plane fly twice as far?

Why it matters

In a steady glide the distance grows with the height: twice the height gives twice the distance if the glide ratio stays the same. The launch itself adds speed, so real results may differ, and that difference is worth measuring.

You need

  • One trimmed plane
  • A tape measure
  • A tall helper
  • Sheet L2

Steps

  1. Predict how far it will fly from each height.
  2. Kneel and launch gently and level, about 0.5 m up. Five times.
  3. Stand and launch from about 1 m. Five times.
  4. A tall helper launches from about 2 m, with the plane held above their head. Five times.
  5. Find the middle distance for each height.
  6. Divide each middle distance by its height.

10.10 The stopwatch challenge

Which launch keeps your plane up longest: a gentle push, or a throw upwards?

Why it matters

Time in the air depends on height and on how slowly the plane sinks. A throw upwards turns the speed of your arm into height, but the plane must then level out into a calm glide. If it loops or stalls at the top, the height is wasted.

You need

  • One plane with broad wings, trimmed to glide
  • Two stopwatches or phones, and two timers
  • A big indoor room with a high ceiling
  • Sheet L2

Steps

  1. Predict which launch will stay up longer.
  2. Both timers start at the release and stop at the first touch. Write down the average of their two times.
  3. Launch five times, gently and level, from shoulder height.
  4. Throw five times upwards, from the same spot.
  5. Find the middle time for each kind of launch.
  6. Tally what each upward throw did: glided, looped or stalled.

Record your launches on sheet L2

Launch12345Middle
Gentle and level

What to look for: Does the upward throw climb, turn over and glide, or loop and drop?

What we know so far: Not yet measured not yet measured. The world record for time aloft is 31.2 s, set in Kunshan, China, in 20262.

Safety: Throw upwards only where the ceiling is high and nothing breakable hangs above. Everyone else stands behind you.

10.11 A wind tunnel in the hallway

What does the air do over a wing when the nose tips up?

Why it matters

A wind tunnel holds a model still and moves the air past it, so the flow is easy to watch. A fan in a hallway cannot make the smooth, even air of a real tunnel, so this test is for seeing, not for measuring speeds. Short tufts of wool show the air: lying flat where it flows smoothly, flapping where it breaks away.

You need

  • A desk fan with a guard
  • One folded plane
  • Five pieces of wool, each 3 cm long, and sticky tape
  • A thread 50 cm long and a paper clip
  • Sheet L2

Steps

  1. Predict when the tufts will start to flap.
  2. Tape the five tufts in a row across the top of one wing, near the back.
  3. Clip the thread under the plane at its balance point. Set the fan on the floor, on its lowest setting.
  4. Hold the thread 1 m in front of the fan so the plane flies like a kite. Look five times, a few seconds each, and tally what you see.
  5. Bend both back edges up 2 mm, then 4 mm, and look five times at each.
  6. Switch the fan off before you touch the plane.

Record your launches on sheet L2

What to look for: Make one tally mark in a column each time you look. Do the flapping marks grow as the nose rises?

What we know so far: Not yet measured not yet measured. Real wind tunnels use the same trick: NASA explains that tufts show where the flow separates, which is what makes a wing stall3.

Safety: Use a fan with a guard, on its lowest setting. Keep fingers, hair, thread and paper away from the guard, and switch the fan off before you touch anything near it.

10.12 Drawing results

A picture of your results shows what a table hides.

The middle of five. Put your five distances in order from shortest to longest. The third is the middle, which scientists call the median. One wild throw cannot move it much, which is why we use it instead of the best throw.

Tally charts. Some results are not numbers. To count what flights did, give each kind of flight a row, such as glided, dived, stalled or turned, and draw one stroke per flight. Every fifth stroke crosses the four before it, so you can count in fives at a glance.

Comparing two setups. Draw a line for distance. Mark each launch of the first setup as a dot above the line and each launch of the second as a cross below it, then circle the two middles. If the dots and crosses overlap a lot, the change made little difference. If they sit apart, it probably mattered.

What “not yet measured” means. In this book an empty result is not a zero. It means nobody has done the test yet. When a recorded trial fills it, we will print the number with its trial ID, the date and the conditions.

Sharing a log. A log helps other people when it says which design and revision you flew, the paper, how you trimmed and launched it, and where. Every launch counts, including the bad ones.

Try it

  1. Take the five distances from one setup.
  2. Write them in order, shortest first.
  3. Circle the third one.

Look for: That is your median.

Go deeper

With five values, the median sets aside the two most extreme results on each side, so a single bad launch does not decide the answer.

If the results of two setups overlap heavily, collect more launches before you conclude anything. Five each is a start, not a proof.
