Can a paper butterfly turn?

Pick a paper monarch, blue morpho or swallowtail. What will happen if you raise just one little tab at the back of its wing? Try the two settings on the same glider and use the landing marks to tell the story. Open the English workshop

Gather one sheet of A4 or US Letter paper, the matching butterfly pattern, scissors, a ruler, a small blunt paper clip, a pencil and six little slips of paper. Find a clear indoor space.

  1. Print at 100% / Actual size. Measure the line marked 50 mm. If the measurement differs, select the matching paper size and turn off page scaling before you print again.
  2. Follow the butterfly’s pictured cut-and-fold guide. An adult can make the small tab cuts. Fit the clip at the marked nose position. Leave both rear tabs level with their wings.
  3. Choose a launch line, such as a seam in the floor. Face the same direction each time. Point to the place you think the butterfly will land. Release it gently three times from the same spot and height. After each flight, mark where it first touched the floor. You can write the flight number on a slip or draw it in a notebook.
  4. Point the nose away from you and raise only the left back tab a little. Keep the clip, wing folds and launch the same. Predict again. Fly three more times and mark each landing, including any that surprised you.
  5. Compare the two sets of marks. Did the raised-tab flights cluster to one side? Which of the six landings is hardest to explain? If the pattern is unclear, say so and try the comparison again.

Write left, ahead or right as seen from the launch line, or draw a dot for each first landing. Nearby dots can overlap. Adults and older children can measure sideways distance from an imaginary straight line through the launch point. A young builder can point, fly and mark the spots while someone else reads and folds.

For the adult leading the activity, a useful closing question is “Which mark makes you think so?” The onscreen trajectory illustrates a prediction. The marks record what this paper model did. To share an observation, include the model, paper size, tab setting and what happened. English workshop · Public source and feedback
