How it flies

Your butterfly is a glider with no tail. Its wings must hold it up and keep it steady, so where it balances matters more than anything else.

Two points matter. The balance point is where it balances on your fingertips. The lift point is where the air pushes up on the wings, on average. On this butterfly our model puts the balance target 33 mm behind the head and the lift point 37 mm behind it: 4 mm apart, balance first. When the balance point is a little in front of the lift point, the butterfly glides steadily. When it is behind, the nose rises, the butterfly slows and drops.

The wings are swept back like a paper dart, about 30° at the front edge. That moves the lift point back, behind the head, so the two clips on the head can sit in front of it. The forewings overlap the hindwings, the way a gliding butterfly holds them.

The tabs at the back set the angle. Bent up a little, they make the air push the tail down, so the nose lifts and the wings can make lift. The V shape of the wings helps it roll back to level.

This is our fourth fold. The earlier ones did not fly well in the founder’s informal tries; those were observations, not recorded trials. This one gets its balance from swept wings instead of a long head. Build it and tell us how it flies.

Make it

What you need 120 g/m² paper, scissors, ruler, two 33 mm clips and a glue stick

  1. Step 1 / 8

    Cut

    Cut the solid outline. Cut the V between each antenna and the dark nose flap, and the four short slits at the back of the hindwings.

    One piece. The nose flap is free from both antennae.

  2. Step 2 / 8

    Score

    Lay a ruler on every dashed line and press along it with an empty ballpoint pen.

    Each fold line shows a groove.

  3. Step 3 / 8

    Fold the front edges

    Fold each tan strip (3) under its forewing on the dash-dot line. A glue stick under the strip makes the wing stiffer.

    The front edge runs straight from the head to the wingtip, doubled.

  4. Step 4 / 8

    Tuck the nose flap

    Fold the dark nose flap (4) under, behind the head, on its dash-dot line. Press hard.

    The flap lies flat behind the head. Its eyes face down.

  5. Step 5 / 8

    Fold the body

    Fold in half on the dashed centre line (5), colors inside. The nose flap wraps round the front of the body.

    The eyes now show on both sides of the head.

  6. Step 6 / 8

    Open the wings

    Fold each wing back out on its dash-dot root line (6). Set a shallow V with the tip-height strip.

    The body is deep at the head and shallow at the tail. Both wingtips are the same height.

  7. Step 7 / 8

    Clip and balance

    Push a clip onto the head from the front, over the nose flap, and a second on top. Rest the wings on two fingertips under the green dots.

    It stays level. Tail drops: add weight at the head. Nose drops: slide a clip back.

  8. Step 8 / 8

    Launch and trim

    Push it smoothly forward, nose level, about as fast as a jog. Dives: bend both hindwing tabs up 1–2 mm. Climbs and stalls: flatten them.

    Turns left: lift the right tab a little (seen from the tail). Turns right: the left tab.

Launch it

Hold the body under the wings between finger and thumb, nose level. Stand still in a room without draughts. Push forward smoothly, about as fast as a jog, and let go. Do not throw it upward. Mark where it first touches the floor.

Trim it

If it…Try this
Dives into the floorBend both tabs up 1–2 mm. Still diving: slide one clip back a little. It must still balance on the dots.
Swoops up, slows, then dropsFlatten both tabs. Check that it balances on the dots.
Turns to one sideBend the tab on the outside of the turn up a little: the right tab for a left turn, the left tab for a right turn.
Drops straight after launchPush a little faster. Then check the tabs.
Rolls over or spiralsCheck both tips on the strip: same height. Then raise both a little.
The wings flap or droopGlue the tan front strips under the wings. Use 120 g/m² paper with the wings along the long side. Try a smaller span.

Field notes

A short tail on each hindwing

The Old World swallowtail lives across much of temperate Europe and Asia, and in parts of North Africa and North America. Its caterpillars eat plants of the carrot family, such as wild angelica.

Look for the short tail on each hindwing, the blue marks beside it and the red spot near the body.

Our paper swallowtail has the most slender wings of the three. Slender wings usually glide flatter.

Try this

Can one tab steer it?

Predict first. Which way will it turn when only the left tab is bent up? Bending a tab up takes a little lift off that side, so our guess is left.

  1. Start with both tabs flat and launch five times from the same spot.
  2. Bend only the left tab up 2 mm.
  3. Launch five more times with the same push.
  4. Mark where each one lands.

What to look for. Whether the second set of marks moves to one side, and which side.

Go deeper

A glider with no tail flies steadily when its balance point is in front of its neutral point, the spot where extra lift acts when the nose tips up. On a flat wing that spot sits about a quarter of the way back from the front edge, averaged over the wing’s area. The gap between the two points, as a share of the wing’s average depth, is called the static margin. Our flat-wing model gives the first fold (K1) a margin of -9% on 80 g/m² with one clip: not steady. It gives this dart version (K3) +15% with two 33 mm clips on 120 g/m² paper. Almost all of that comes from the sweep; the folded nose and deep body add a little, and the doubled front edges are there to stiffen the swept wings.

Being steady is not enough. A perfectly flat wing balanced in front of its neutral point can settle only where it makes no lift, so it dives. Bending the tabs at the back up a little, which engineers call reflex, makes the air push the tail down, so the wing glides nose-up and makes lift. Tailless aircraft solve the same problem with bent-up back edges, swept wings and twisted wings. Early in aviation, Igo Etrich and Franz Wels built a tailless glider modelled on the winged seed of the vine Alsomitra macrocarpa, then called Zanonia.

Each square metre of wing holds up about 3.0 newtons of weight (3.0 N/m²). At a lift coefficient of 0.6, a measure of how hard the wing works, that predicts a glide near 2.9 m/s, about a jog, which is why the launch is a smooth push at jogging speed, not a walk. Paper bends: with the grain along the span, the model predicts the tips bend about 10 mm in a steady glide (1 g) on 120 g/m² and about 22 mm on 80 g/m². Every number here is a model estimate, not a measurement.

Aspect ratio, a measure of how long and slim a wing is, is span squared divided by wing area. The model gives 4.4 here, against 4.0 for the monarch and 3.4 for the morpho. A higher aspect ratio means less induced drag, the drag from the swirls at the wingtips, for the same lift. At these low speeds and sizes, friction drag from air rubbing on the paper is also large, so the advantage may be small. Compare the three on the same paper.

Earlier folds

K3, on this page, is our fourth butterfly. The earlier folds were rear wing tabs (W1), a pinched spine (K1) and a folded front edge with the clips on a long head (K2). They did not fly well in the founder’s informal tries, so we no longer offer them; books and links that already use them still open. Those were observations, not recorded trials, and K3 has no recorded flight yet either.
