How it flies

Your butterfly is a glider with no tail. Its wings must hold it up and keep it steady, so where it balances matters more than anything else.

Two points matter. The balance point is where it balances on your fingertips. The lift point is where the air pushes up on the wings, on average. On this butterfly our model puts the balance target 39 mm behind the nose and the lift point 45 mm behind it: 6 mm apart, balance first. When the balance point is a little in front of the lift point, the butterfly glides steadily. When it is behind, the nose rises, the butterfly slows and drops.

The two clips on the head move the balance point forward. The long head works as a lever, so two light clips are enough. Real butterflies do not have long necks; ours needs one to balance.

The tabs at the back set the angle. Bent up a little, they make the air push the tail down, so the nose lifts and the wings can make lift. The V shape of the wings helps it roll back to level.

This is an experimental glider, our second attempt. Our first fold glided poorly in two early builds by the founder; that was an observation, not a recorded trial. This version moves the balance forward. Nobody has flown it in a recorded test yet. Build it and tell us how it flies.

Make it

What you need 120 g/m² paper, scissors, ruler and two 33 mm clips

  1. Step 1 / 8

    Cut

    Cut the outside line, including the edge strips and the antenna clubs. Cut the four short slits at the back of the hindwings.

    One piece. Each hindwing has a tab between two slits.

  2. Step 2 / 8

    Score

    Lay a ruler on each dashed line and press along it with an empty ballpoint pen.

    Every fold line shows a groove. Thick paper now folds cleanly.

  3. Step 3 / 8

    Fold the costa

    Fold each edge strip under its wing along its dashed line. Press it flat.

    The front edge is straight and doubled, like the costa vein of a real wing.

  4. Step 4 / 8

    Fold the body

    Fold in half on the dashed centre line, colors inside. Then fold each wing back out on its dash-dot line.

    The two body layers hang underneath and touch.

  5. Step 5 / 8

    Clip the head

    Slide both clips onto the head over the two body layers, one on top of the other, all the way to the nose.

    The clips stay inside the dashed clip box.

  6. Step 6 / 8

    Balance

    Rest the wings on two fingertips under the ringed dots. Tail drops: add a clip. Nose drops: slide a clip back, or remove one.

    It stays level on your fingers.

  7. Step 7 / 8

    Set the V

    Lift both wings into a shallow V. Look from the tail and compare each wingtip with the strip.

    Both tips are the same height.

  8. Step 8 / 8

    Launch and trim

    Push it smoothly forward, nose level, about as fast as a jog. Dives: bend both tabs up 1–2 mm. Swoops up and stalls: flatten them.

    Turns left: bend the right tab up a little. Turns right: the left tab.

Launch it

Hold the body under the wings between finger and thumb, nose level. Stand still in a room without draughts. Push forward smoothly, about as fast as a jog, and let go. Do not throw it upward. Mark where it first touches the floor.

Trim it

If it…Try this
Dives into the floorBend both tabs up 1–2 mm. Still diving: slide one clip back a little. It must still balance on the dots.
Swoops up, slows, then dropsFlatten both tabs. Check that it balances on the dots.
Turns to one sideBend the tab on the outside of the turn up a little: the right tab for a left turn, the left tab for a right turn.
Drops straight after launchPush a little faster. Then check the tabs.
Rolls over or spiralsCheck both tips on the strip: same height. Then raise both a little.
The wings flap or droopUse 120 g/m² paper with the wings along the long side. Try a smaller span.

Field notes

A short tail on each hindwing

The Old World swallowtail lives across much of temperate Europe and Asia, and in parts of North Africa and North America. Its caterpillars eat plants of the carrot family, such as wild angelica.

Look for the short tail on each hindwing, the blue marks beside it and the red spot near the body.

Our paper swallowtail has the most slender wings of the three. Slender wings usually glide flatter.

Kizhi Museum Reserve · Swallowtail ↗

Try this

Can one tab steer it?

Predict first. Which way will it turn when only the left tab is bent up? Bending a tab up takes a little lift off that side, so our guess is left.

  1. Start with both tabs flat and launch five times from the same spot.
  2. Bend only the left tab up 2 mm.
  3. Launch five more times with the same push.
  4. Mark where each one lands.

What to look for. Whether the second set of marks moves to one side, and which side.

Go deeper

A glider with no tail flies steadily when its balance point is in front of its neutral point, the spot where extra lift acts when the nose tips up. On a flat wing that spot sits about a quarter of the way back from the front edge, averaged over the wing’s area. The gap between the two points, as a share of the wing’s average depth, is called the static margin. Our flat-wing model gives the first fold (K1) a margin of -9% on 80 g/m² with one clip: not steady. It gives this second version (K2) +14% with two 33 mm clips on 120 g/m² paper.

Being steady is not enough. A perfectly flat wing balanced in front of its neutral point can settle only where it makes no lift, so it dives. Bending the tabs at the back up a little, which engineers call reflex, makes the air push the tail down, so the wing glides nose-up and makes lift. Tailless aircraft solve the same problem with bent-up back edges, swept wings and twisted wings. Early in aviation, Igo Etrich and Franz Wels built a tailless glider modelled on the winged seed of the vine Alsomitra macrocarpa, then called Zanonia.

Each square metre of wing holds up about 2.6 newtons of weight (2.6 N/m²). At a lift coefficient of 0.6, a measure of how hard the wing works, that predicts a glide near 2.7 m/s, about a jog, which is why the launch is a smooth push at jogging speed, not a walk. Paper bends: with the grain along the span, the model predicts the tips bend about 8 mm in a steady glide (1 g) on 120 g/m² and about 18 mm on 80 g/m². Every number here is a model estimate, not a measurement.

Aspect ratio, a measure of how long and slim a wing is, is span squared divided by wing area. The model gives 3.9 here, against 3.6 for the monarch and 2.4 for the morpho. A higher aspect ratio means less induced drag, the drag from the swirls at the wingtips, for the same lift. At these low speeds and sizes, friction drag from air rubbing on the paper is also large, so the advantage may be small. Compare the three on the same paper.
